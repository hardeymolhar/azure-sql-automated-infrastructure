# Working Principles

## Engineering Discipline

1. **Think before coding.** State your assumptions out loud. If the request is ambiguous, ask. If a
   simpler approach exists, push back. Stop when you are confused, name what is unclear, and do not
   just pick one interpretation and run.

2. **Simplicity first.** Write the minimum code that solves the problem. No speculative abstractions.
   No flexibility nobody asked for. The test: would a senior engineer call this overcomplicated?

3. **Surgical changes.** Touch only what the task requires. Do not improve neighboring code. Do not
   refactor what is not broken. Every changed line should trace back to the request.

4. **Goal-driven execution.** Turn vague instructions into verifiable targets before writing a line.
   "Add validation" becomes "write tests for invalid inputs, then make them pass."

## Becoming a Great Project Documenter

To hone in on the best practices for articulating project decisions, look at what makes a design
successful — that forms the basis for how we communicate about it.

Understand the fundamentals of what makes a great experience, so you can reproduce that thinking and
approach.

### The Big Three

**What makes a good design good?**

The difference between a good designer and a great designer is the ability not only to solve the
problem, but also to articulate how the project solves it in a way that is compelling and fosters
agreement.

Every design needs three things to be successful:

1. **It solves a problem.**
2. **It's easy for users.**
3. **It's supported by everyone.**

Follow these basics to create a great UX.

Find out which factors matter most to your stakeholders (e.g., impressions, conversions), then pick
one or two measurable issues you'd like to improve.

> For every decision and change, constantly ask yourself:
> **"What problem am I trying to solve with this?"**

### Make It Easy — 1/3

Usability is the core challenge of design, but it can be difficult to explain that to other people.
Just as you solve problems and make yourself aware of your own decisions, you must do the same here.

### Make It Happen — 2/3

To communicate successfully about our work, we must be able to answer three questions:

1. What problem does it solve?
2. How does it affect the user?
3. Why is it better than the alternatives?

Your ability to be thoughtful about a problem and articulate a solution matters more than your
ability to design the perfect solution or generate the perfect code every time.
